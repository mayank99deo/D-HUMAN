# Preventive health checkups in India: what the numbers say

*Written with AI assistance and edited by Mayank Narayan.*

Ask someone in India how their health is, and they'll usually tell you how they feel. That's a fair answer, and an incomplete one. The conditions that now weigh most on India's health build slowly and quietly. You can't feel your blood sugar creeping up or your haemoglobin sliding. You can measure them.

This post puts real numbers on that shift, from national surveys and official data, each with its source. Then it lays out a simple way to catch drift early: check a few markers regularly, pay attention to what you eat, and take anything outside range to a doctor.

## India's health burden has shifted

Infections and undernutrition still matter in India, but most deaths now come from a different group of conditions. The World Health Organization's country profile for India estimated that noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), such as heart disease, cancers, chronic lung disease and diabetes, account for 63% of all deaths. Infections, maternal and newborn conditions and undernutrition together accounted for an estimated 26% (2018 profile, 2016 data).

The largest national study of metabolic health shows how far the risk has spread. The ICMR-INDIAB study measured 1,13,043 adults aged 20 and over, in towns and villages across 31 states, union territories and the National Capital Territory, between 2008 and 2020. Its national report, published in *The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology* in 2023, found these shares of adults with each condition:

- **Diabetes:** 11.4%
- **Prediabetes:** 15.3%
- **High blood pressure (hypertension):** 35.5%
- **Generalised obesity:** 28.6%
- **Abdominal obesity, or excess fat around the waist:** 39.5%
- **Dyslipidaemia, an unhealthy pattern of blood fats such as cholesterol and triglycerides:** 81.2%

Put plainly: about one adult in nine has diabetes, more have prediabetes than diabetes, and more than one in three has high blood pressure. It isn't only a city story, either. Every condition in the study was more common in urban areas except prediabetes.

## Most of it builds without symptoms

Here's the hard part: you can't feel most of these. The WHO's fact sheet on hypertension says it plainly: "Most people with hypertension don't feel any symptoms." It adds that "the only way to detect hypertension is to have a health professional measure blood pressure."

So it goes unnoticed. WHO's 2025 hypertension profile for India estimates that 210.2 million adults aged 30 to 79 have high blood pressure. Only 39% of them have been diagnosed, and only 17% have it under control.

Blood tells a similar story. The National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019–21) tested haemoglobin across the country and found that 57% of women and 25% of men aged 15 to 49 were anaemic. That's more than half of women in that age group, picked up by a simple haemoglobin test.

Vitamin D is the same. Research across 2.2 lakh lab tests in 27 Indian cities found that 76 of every 100 people tested were deficient (2023).

None of these are rare, and none of them announce themselves. They show up in a reading or a report, which is why measuring beats guessing. If you already have a recent report, start there: here's how to get [a blood test report read in plain words](https://www.d-human.fit/blood-report-analysis).

## Care is still paid for largely at home

When something does need care, much of the bill lands on the family. India's latest National Health Accounts, for 2023–24 and published by the Health Ministry in 2026, found that households paid 42.7% of the country's total health spending out of their own pockets. That share has come down a long way from 64.2% in 2013–14, but it's still more than ₹2 of every ₹5 spent on health in India.

Knowing your numbers early won't stop every bill. It does give you, and your doctor, more time and more choices.

## India already screens from 30

India's public health system is built on the same idea. Under the National Health Mission, people over 30 are targeted for screening for five common NCDs: high blood pressure, diabetes, and oral, breast and cervical cancer. Ayushman Arogya Mandirs, the renamed Health and Wellness Centres, bring these services closer to home. The Health Ministry reported 1,81,873 of them running by 30 November 2025.

If you're over 30 and haven't had your blood pressure and blood sugar checked recently, your nearest centre is one place to start.

## A simple way to catch drift early

You don't need a lab every month. You need a baseline, a habit, and a rule for what to do with results.

1. **Know your numbers once.** Blood pressure, plus a blood report that covers blood sugar, blood fats (a lipid profile) and a blood count with haemoglobin. These are the markers behind the national numbers above. Ask your doctor whether vitamin D, vitamin B12 or iron studies make sense for you too.
2. **Read each result against your lab's printed range.** Ranges differ between labs and methods, so the range printed beside your result is the one that applies. Note where each marker sits: comfortably in range, at the edge, or outside.
3. **Watch the trend, not one number.** A single result is a snapshot. Two reports side by side show which way a marker is moving.
4. **Take anything outside range to your own doctor.** Don't start medicines or high-dose supplements on your own.
5. **Retest on purpose.** Recheck the markers that were outside or at the edge of range when your doctor suggests, and compare them with the last report.

**Worth showing a doctor:** any result outside your lab's printed range, blood pressure that reads high on more than one day, or any symptom that worries you. If you feel unwell, don't wait for a test.

## Where food fits

Many of these markers are tied to what you eat, day after day. ICMR-NIN's Dietary Guidelines for Indians (2024) put it directly: "Estimates show that 56.4% of total disease burden in India is due to unhealthy diets."

Food isn't the whole story. But it's the part of your health you shape several times a day, and the easiest part to lose track of. Indian meals make that harder: a katori of dal, two rotis and a spoon of ghee add up differently in every kitchen. Writing meals down, even roughly, shows you patterns that memory won't. ## Questions

### What is a preventive health checkup?

A set of measurements taken while you feel well: usually blood pressure, weight and waist, and a blood report. The point is to spot markers drifting before anything feels wrong, and to have a baseline to compare with later. It isn't a diagnosis. Results outside range go to your doctor.

### At what age should I start health screening in India?

India's national programme targets adults over 30 for screening for high blood pressure, diabetes and three common cancers. Your doctor may suggest starting earlier or testing more, for example if close family members have diabetes or heart disease.

### How often should I get my blood tested?

There's no single answer for everyone. A sensible pattern is a baseline, then rechecks of the markers that were outside or at the edge of range, on the schedule your doctor suggests. Two reports compared tell you more than one read alone.

*This is general information, not medical advice. If you feel unwell, see a doctor.*
